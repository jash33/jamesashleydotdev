## Taste is Dynamic

Almost everyone can relate to some variation of the following experience: You
hear a song at home or by yourself, and you love it. You can't wait to share it
with somebody. When you get a chance to, you find that in the moment that you're
sharing it with another person, the way you experience the song is different
from how you perceived it when you were alone. The same can be true in the
opposite direction - sometimes you can enjoy a song with friends that you
wouldn't like if you were by yourself.

It's not intuitive that this would be true, even though most of us know that it
is from experience. Your tastes haven't suddenly changed just because other
people are in the room. Why do you feel differently?

## Collective Sensibility

Well, assuming that you're a person with a healthy amount of empathy, your
tastes HAVE changed in that moment - somewhat. Human beings have a lot of
psychological, perhaps evolutionary, tendencies towards thinking as a group and
as a community when we are in the presence of other people. We can be perhaps
more like a hivemind than we realize.

It turns out appealing to this "hivemind" is one of the most commonly needed -
and difficult to develop - skills that exists. Human work often involves a need
to create something with broad appeal - a buisness, a brand, a product, a
conference talk, a commercial. How can we create for the masses when we only
have ourselves, and perhaps our team, involved in creating the work?

It certainly is not easy. In the case of music, the larger the size of the
audience gets, the fewer and fewer musical artists are able to appeal to that
increasingly broad sensibility - only a handful of songs every year are hits.
However, some artists are able to continually tap into this universal
sensibility year after year. I call this creating for Universal Taste - the
emergent aesthetic beats that humanity largely agrees upon.

## Developing Universal Taste

While most of us are not born with an innate level of talent creating for
Universal Taste, I believe there are some ways that we learn to tune our tastes
to tap into that collective sensibility, that universal taste, when we need to.

The most important thing to do is probably not to create your product in a
silo - just like how the agile approach to developing software depends mostly on
frequent demos with the customer, or how the Beatles spent 10,000 hours as
teenagers in England and Germany, playing shows constantly and having a
continuous iterative feedback cycle with their audience.

The next best thing you can do is probably to inundate yourself with products
that have already proven that they do appeal to that universal sensibility. I've
found that listening to The Beatles or music with a similarly "universal"
aesthetic before I give conference talks helps me better connect with the room -
in ways I can't explain.
